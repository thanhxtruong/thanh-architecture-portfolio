Case 01 / Engineering deep dive / Illustrative evidence

The hard part was deciding what the system was allowed to do when neither the customer nor the platform knew whether a payment had succeeded.

Business context & constraints

The marketplace processed approximately 80,000 payments per day. During provider incidents, payment operations spent a median of 22 hours a week reconciling orders manually. The provider was contractually fixed for another year; checkout availability and audit history had to be preserved.

My contribution & the team’s

I traced failed requests through logs, payment attempts, and provider records. I implemented the recovery worker, provider adapter, and integration tests, pairing with another engineer on persistence boundaries. I proposed the state model and rollout gates; the team’s architect reviewed the guarantees. Checkout engineers owned the API and customer messaging, while finance and operations defined reconciliation acceptance criteria.

From diagnosis to implementation

I reproduced the incident with a provider simulator that committed a capture and dropped its response. Correlating the persisted intent ID with the provider ledger exposed the gap: the retry path treated a missing response as permission to start a new attempt identity.

I separated provider transport, durable recovery state, and local order completion behind narrow interfaces. The worker routed uncertain results into reconciliation; it never interpreted an empty local result as evidence that no external payment existed. I kept the first implementation within the existing service and deployed it behind a cohort flag.

During review, a teammate found that the generic HTTP retry handler could resend captures before the worker observed the failure. We changed the capture client's retry configuration and added a transport-level test. I documented that distinction so future resilience changes would preserve the provider contract.

The guarantee has a boundary

We persisted the provider key before the first send and reused it for retries of the same payment intent. This depended on the provider’s documented key scope and 24-hour deduplication window. Unknown outcomes entered provider-status reconciliation. Once the safe retry window elapsed, automated capture stopped and the item required review. We made no end-to-end “exactly once” claim.

Provider confirmation and local order completion were separate states. A successful provider call followed by a database failure retried only the local completion once confirmation had been recorded. Where confirmation could not be recorded, recovery queried the provider using the persisted intent identity.

How we reached agreement

Product wanted an immediate yes/no result; finance could not accept a second capture. I facilitated a failure walkthrough with both teams and proposed a third customer-visible state: “Payment confirmation pending.” We agreed on a confirmation-time objective, a customer notification path, and an operations queue before implementation started.

Rollout and validation

  1. Ran reconciliation in shadow mode against historical incidents and compared results with the finance ledger.
  2. Injected lost responses, worker termination, expired leases, duplicate callbacks, and database failures around each persistence boundary.
  3. Moved traffic through 1%, 10%, 50%, and 100% cohorts. Each step required 48 hours inside the error and queue-age gates.
  4. Rollback stopped new-cohort assignment. Existing intents continued through their original recovery flow; we never routed them back to the legacy capture path.

What changed after launch

Over the first 90 days at full traffic, manual recovery effort fell from 22 to 2 hours per week (medians across the measured weeks). Daily ledger reconciliation found no duplicate captures attributable to retries across 7.2 million payment intents. Zero observed duplicates is a measurement over this period, not proof that duplicates are impossible.

A regional outage still generated enough unknown outcomes to exceed the review team’s capacity. We added queue-age alerts and an escalation threshold; we also documented who could authorize recovery after the provider window expired.

What I would do earlier: Define the operational ownership and evidence retention before selecting a retry policy. The recovery queue was part of the product, and needed the same design attention as checkout.

Case 02 / 3-minute read

The constraint shaped the scope

A nine-year-old logistics application supported 220 enterprise customers. Five teams shared its database and deployment window. The business could fund one team for six months, while customer commitments continued. I proposed tracking as the first extraction because its ownership and read traffic were comparatively well understood. The staff engineer led the wider program; I owned implementation and delivery for the tracking scope.

What we deliberately deferred

We rejected a whole-platform rewrite: its first customer benefit arrived too late. We also rejected splitting each database entity into a service. Tracking became the first independent deployment; invoicing and pricing remained in the existing application.

What I implemented and tested

I built the tracking endpoints and a compatibility adapter that preserved the legacy response contract. I wrote checkpointed backfill tooling, added contract tests for older clients, and used query plans to investigate a slow history endpoint before canary traffic increased.

I paired with two engineers on fixtures and migration scripts, then reviewed their changes against the same compatibility cases. The staff engineer reviewed the extraction boundary; I remained responsible for the tracking code and production follow-up.

A reversible migration

An adapter preserved the existing contract. We backfilled tracking history, compared shadow responses, and moved reads through customer cohorts. A single authoritative writer was designated at each stage. Cutover used a checkpoint and reconciliation gate before the new writer was enabled. Once new writes began, rollback required replay and reconciliation, not just a traffic switch.

Leadership contribution

I surfaced the proposed service’s operational needs during design review and helped our team lead agree on responsibility with platform engineers. Our tracking team accepted lifecycle ownership; platform provided deployment tooling. I wrote the first runbook, paired on incident drills, and reviewed the migration risks with the program’s staff engineer each week.

What the evidence showed

For the migrated tracking scope, median commit-to-production lead time declined from 14 days to 3 days across two 12-week windows. No planned customer downtime was required. The measure excluded emergency fixes and did not establish a platform-wide improvement.

The new boundary increased operational work: another deployment, dashboards, and an on-call surface. We deferred the second extraction until the team demonstrated it could operate the first reliably.

Lesson: An extraction is complete when the team can change and operate it independently. A new service alone does not establish independence.

Case 03 / 3-minute read

The business problem

Enterprise customers needed consistent tenant roles across four products. Permission changes required separate implementations, and support could not explain why the same person had different access in different interfaces.

Separate shared policy from enforcement

We compared one synchronous authorization service with a shared policy distribution model. We chose versioned policy bundles and local enforcement for ordinary reads, accepting a bounded propagation delay to avoid a new synchronous dependency on every request. Sensitive administrative operations required an online check and failed closed if it was unavailable.

Where the boundary matters

Tenant identity came from the authenticated request context, never from a caller-selected policy identifier. Ordinary-read bundles expired after five minutes; stale or unavailable bundles denied access. Emergency revocation was immediate for online-checked operations and bounded by bundle expiry for ordinary reads. Security and product explicitly accepted this distinction.

What I implemented

I built the policy validator and request middleware with injectable clock and policy-source interfaces. Tests covered expiry boundaries, tenant mismatches, incompatible versions, and the required online-check failure. I packaged the library, wrote an integration example, and paired with engineers from the first two adopting products.

Code review exposed a caching risk: keys based only on role name could mix tenants. I changed the cache identity to include tenant and policy version and added a regression case. Security reviewed the overall trust model; the implementation stayed my responsibility.

Helping others deliver

Product teams disagreed about who could define roles. I used testable customer scenarios to make the differences concrete in a workshop led by security. I documented the resulting contracts and helped two engineers adopt the library. Security owned minimum controls; each product owned resource-specific permissions; platform owned distribution and compatibility.

Validation and outcomes

We tested cross-tenant access, stale policies, version skew, missing claims, and unavailable policy services. Across six follow-on integrations, median implementation time was two weeks, compared with six weeks across the four initial bespoke integrations. The small and non-equivalent samples limit the comparison.

The first shared role vocabulary was too broad for delegated billing administrators. We added a scoped permission rather than extending the global administrator role. That decision is captured as a revision to the policy contract.

Accepted cost: Local enforcement reduces runtime coupling but adds a compatibility and distribution problem. The design requires expiry behavior and contract tests to stay explicit.

IMPLEMENTATION / C# · PAYMENTS

Keep uncertain recovery explicit

A focused excerpt connects the failure model to control flow.

What I built: Recovery routing, provider adapter, persisted attempt identity, and resumable local completion. This abbreviated example shows the routing decision; types, persistence internals, leasing, and error handling are omitted.

// Illustrative C# excerpt: dispatch policy, not the full worker.
// The intent identity is persisted before this method runs.
async Task RecoverAsync(PaymentIntent intent, CancellationToken ct)
{
    var result = await provider.LookupAsync(intent.ProviderKey, ct);

    switch (result)
    {
        case Confirmed confirmation:
            // Persist provider confirmation; local completion is resumable.
            await recovery.RecordConfirmationAsync(intent.Id, confirmation, ct);
            await orders.CompleteFromConfirmationAsync(intent.Id, ct);
            return;

        case DefinitivelyAbsent when intent.WithinSafeRetryWindow(clock.UtcNow):
            // Same persisted key, never a newly generated identity.
            await dispatcher.ScheduleSameIntentAsync(intent.Id, ct);
            return;

        default:
            // Includes ambiguous lookup and expired retry window.
            await recovery.RequireReviewAsync(intent.Id, ct);
            return;
    }
}

Review points: Provider-specific semantics belong in the adapter. “Definitively absent” requires a provider-supported authoritative lookup, not a generic 404. Scheduling and local completion must tolerate repetition. Lookup errors remain unresolved and observable; they never authorize a new payment identity.

Boundary: A lookup does not eliminate a race by itself. The same persisted provider key and its deduplication contract remain necessary when a capture is retried. Once that contract's safe window ends, the system requires review.

REGRESSION TEST / RESPONSE LOSS

Reproduce the incident before fixing it

Assert both the external side effect and local recovery result.

Scenario: The provider captures a payment, the response is lost, and the worker restarts. The test verifies that recovery completes the original intent without producing another capture.

// Illustrative xUnit-style scenario; helper types are omitted.
[Fact]
public async Task Lost_response_does_not_create_a_new_capture()
{
    var intent = await fixture.CreatePersistedIntentAsync();
    provider.CaptureThenDropResponseOnce();

    await fixture.DispatchAsync(intent.Id);
    Assert.Equal(PaymentState.Unknown,
                 await fixture.StateOfAsync(intent.Id));

    await fixture.RestartWorkerAndRecoverAsync(intent.Id);

    Assert.Single(provider.CapturesFor(intent.ProviderKey));
    Assert.Equal(PaymentState.Completed,
                 await fixture.StateOfAsync(intent.Id));
}

Test setup: The illustrative fixture uses a real PostgreSQL test instance, a restarted worker instance, and a deterministic provider simulator. Reusing persisted state across the restart matters; reusing in-memory worker state would miss the recovery boundary.

What it establishes: The worker responds correctly to this modeled failure. The simulator's behavior must be checked separately against the provider's documented contract and sandbox. This test alone does not establish real-provider deduplication guarantees.

DELIVERY / TRACKING MIGRATION

Cutover gates and recovery ownership

A migration plan includes the point where rollback becomes harder.

  1. Backfill gate: Record counts and content checksums match the agreed checkpoint. Data engineering signs off.
  2. Shadow gate: Seven days of response comparison; explain every discrepancy affecting customer-visible state. Tracking team owns disposition.
  3. Read cutover: Customer cohorts increase only while latency and error budgets remain within baseline. On-call may restore legacy reads.
  4. Write cutover: Pause new intake briefly behind a durable queue; drain old writes; reconcile the checkpoint; switch the authoritative writer; resume intake.
  5. After new writes: Returning to the old writer requires reconciliation and replay of the new writes. Incident command must approve the recovery plan.
  6. Retirement: Remove the legacy path only after a 30-day observation period and customer-support sign-off.

Accountability: Tracking owns the service and runbook. Platform owns deployment support. I chaired the readiness review and recorded unresolved risks; the engineering director authorized the cutover.

CONTRACT / AUTHORIZATION

Permission boundaries you can test

The policy contract specifies denial behavior as carefully as access.

  • Inputs: Authenticated subject, trusted tenant identity, action, resource identity, and supported policy version.
  • Isolation: Subject and resource tenant must match the policy scope before permissions are evaluated.
  • Freshness: Ordinary-read bundles have a maximum five-minute lifetime. Expired or missing policy denies access.
  • Sensitive actions: Role changes, billing administration, and exports require an online authorization check.
  • Failure: Invalid signatures, unsupported versions, uncertain expiry, or failed required online checks deny access.
  • Audit: Record decision ID, policy version, action, resource, result, and reason. Apply the agreed retention and privacy controls.

Representative contract test: A permitted user from tenant A requests a tenant B resource. The result is a denial even if both tenants use a role with the same name.

03 / Decision records

The reasoning behind the code.

Design choices from the projects above. What we accepted, what we gave up, and when to revisit.

ADR–014Represent uncertain payments explicitly.Atlas Market · Reliability / data consistencyAccepted · Jan 2025

Context: A provider may capture a payment even when the caller receives no response. Treating every timeout as a failed payment permits unsafe recovery behavior.

Decision: Persist the payment identity before execution, use the provider’s deduplication contract within its supported window, and reconcile unknown outcomes before further action. Track external confirmation separately from local order completion.

Alternatives: Synchronous retries alone do not resolve uncertainty. Replacing the provider exceeded the business deadline.

Consequences: Customers may wait for confirmation. Operations owns a review queue. The team must monitor age and retain a durable audit of attempts and outcomes.

Revisit when: The provider’s key semantics change; unknown outcomes exceed 0.1% for a day; or the oldest unresolved item exceeds 30 minutes. These trigger review and escalation, not automatic relaxation of the safety policy.

Advice received: Finance defined reconciliation evidence; security reviewed payload retention; checkout product approved the pending state. I proposed the design and implemented the recovery flow; the team architect reviewed the guarantees, and the payments lead and finance operations lead approved rollout readiness.

ADR–009Extract tracking; keep pricing together.Northstar Logistics · Boundaries / modernizationRevised · Oct 2023

Original proposal: Extract both tracking and pricing into independent services during the first migration phase.

New evidence: Dependency tracing showed pricing changes spanned invoicing and customer contracts. Its apparent technical boundary did not match the business transaction boundary.

Revision: Extract tracking only. Keep pricing and invoicing together while introducing explicit internal interfaces and ownership.

Accepted cost: Some shared-release coordination remains. We traded short-term architectural uniformity for a smaller and more reversible delivery scope.

Revisit when: Pricing has independent ownership, known transaction semantics, and evidence that its release coupling materially constrains delivery. Service count is not a success measure.

My part: I withdrew my original recommendation after two engineers presented the dependency evidence, and worked with the staff engineer and product lead to reduce the tracking team’s implementation scope.

ADR–021Distribute policy; enforce at the boundary.Meridian Software · Security / availabilityAccepted · Mar 2023

Context: A shared synchronous policy service would create an availability dependency across four products. Fully independent authorization would preserve inconsistent customer behavior.

Decision: Share signed, versioned policy bundles and evaluate ordinary reads locally. Require online authorization for sensitive administrative operations.

Accepted cost: Ordinary-read revocation may lag by up to the five-minute bundle lifetime. Expired, untrusted, incompatible, or missing bundles deny access. This behavior was reviewed with security and product.

Validation: Tenant mismatch, expiry, signature failure, clock handling, incompatible versions, and loss of the distribution service are explicit contract cases.

Revisit when: Customers require immediate read-access revocation or a product cannot enforce the expiration boundary reliably.
